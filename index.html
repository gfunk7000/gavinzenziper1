Blog 1: A Phenomenon in My World

The phenomenon I want to explore is the way first-year college students around me use clinical mental-health language to describe ordinary academic, social, and adjustment stress. I constantly hear people say that they are “depressed,” have “anxiety,” or are “OCD” when they are talking about things like being overwhelmed with homework, worrying about a test, having a bad week, or being stressed about a social situation. I chose this phenomenon because it is something I have noticed repeatedly since coming to college, rather than something I only read about online. At first, I was thinking more broadly about the medicalization of everyday stress, but that felt too broad. What interested me more was the actual language people around me use and why clinical terms have become such a normal part of everyday conversation.

One of the strongest patterns I have noticed is how casually these terms are used. Someone might say that a messy room is “giving them OCD,” that they are “depressed” because of an assignment, or that they have “anxiety” before an exam. My current interpretation is that mental-health language has become one of the main ways college students explain uncomfortable emotions or stressful experiences. That does not necessarily mean people are pretending that mental-health conditions are real. Instead, it may show how familiar clinical language has become in everyday culture. The line between describing normal emotional stress and describing a diagnosed mental-health condition can become less clear when the same words are constantly used for both.

There are assumptions in that interpretation that I still need to question. I am assuming that students are influenced by things like social media, advertising, and popular culture, but I do not yet know how much those things actually affect the way students speak. I also cannot assume that every person using this language is using it incorrectly because I do not know what someone has been diagnosed with or what they are experiencing privately.

That leaves me with several questions. Why has clinical mental-health language become so common in ordinary conversation? How much do social media and pharmaceutical advertising affect the words people use to describe stress? Does using this language make people more aware of mental health, or can it sometimes make the differences between everyday stress and mental-health disorders harder to recognize? What changes when people begin understanding ordinary experiences through medical or diagnostic language?

Three popular culture artifacts I could use are a Zoloft advertisement about social anxiety disorder, mental-health content on TikTok or Instagram that describes common behaviors using diagnostic terms, and scenes from television or movies where characters describe ordinary emotions through the language of mental illness.

The Zoloft advertisement is probably my strongest artifact because it shows how a company takes feelings that viewers might recognize, such as nervousness or discomfort in social situations, and connects them to a named medical condition and a treatment. Using the ideas from “The Americanization of Mental Illness” as a lens could complicate my interpretation because it suggests that the way people understand mental distress is shaped by the cultural explanations available to them.

Looking at the Zoloft advertisement through that lens makes me wonder whether students are simply using mental-health vocabulary casually, or whether they have learned to understand normal emotions through categories that have become common in American culture.

Tentative XY Thesis: While first-year college students' use of clinical mental-health language could simply be a convenient way of describing ordinary stress and emotions, examining pharmaceutical advertising and other popular representations of mental health suggests that this language may also reflect a broader cultural tendency to interpret everyday experiences through medical and diagnostic categories.

Inquiry Notebook

My inquiry started with observations about how people around me talk about stress, anxiety, depression, and OCD in everyday situations. I noticed that these words are often used in situations involving homework, exams, relationships, social pressure, or adjusting to college.

Patterns I Noticed

  • Clinical mental-health terms are often used casually in everyday conversation.
  • The same words can describe both diagnosed mental-health conditions and ordinary stress.
  • Social media, advertisements, and popular culture frequently use diagnostic mental-health language.
  • Medical language can change the way people understand or describe ordinary emotional experiences.

Questions I Want to Investigate

  • Why has clinical mental-health language become so common among college students?
  • How much do advertising and social media shape the way students describe their emotions?
  • Does this language increase mental-health awareness, blur important distinctions, or potentially do both?
  • What happens when people begin interpreting ordinary stress through diagnostic categories?
